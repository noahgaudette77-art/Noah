Things worth knowing before you buy anything old — written by the people who mend it.

Tailoring · 6 min · March

How to read a jacket before you try it on

Almost everything you need to know about a jacket is visible while it is still on the hanger. Start with the shoulder. A good one follows the line of a real body: a slight, soft roll where the sleeve head meets the shoulder seam, no dimpling, no rigid pad holding a shape the wearer does not have. If the shoulder is wrong, nothing a tailor does will save it. It is the one measurement that cannot be altered.

Then the lapel. Roll it back with your thumb. A canvassed jacket has a lapel with a gentle, three-dimensional curve that returns when released; a fused jacket has a lapel that creases flat like a card. Fusing fails eventually — it bubbles, and there is no repair. Half-canvassed is the honest middle ground and covers most of what we sell.

Look at the buttonholes next. Hand-worked holes have a slightly irregular, dense gimp and a tiny keyhole that flares toward the edge. Machine holes are perfectly even. Neither is a flaw, but hand-worked holes tell you the rest of the jacket was probably made with the same patience.

Finally, the inside. A maker’s ticket in the breast pocket, often with a date and a customer name, is the closest thing tailoring has to a birth certificate. Check the seam allowances while you are in there: a generous allowance means the jacket can be let out, which means a jacket that fits nearly right is a jacket that will fit exactly right for about eighty pounds.

Cloth · 8 min · February

Selvedge, hidden rivets, and other honest tells

Denim is the most counterfeited category in vintage, largely because the people who care about it care very much and the details are small. Fortunately, the tells are physical rather than cosmetic, and a reproduction rarely bothers with all of them.

Turn the outseam out. A narrow shuttle loom finishes its own edge, and that finished edge — the selvedge — appears as a clean, tightly woven band, usually with a coloured line running through it. Wide modern looms cannot produce it; a serged, overlocked outseam means the denim was woven after roughly 1980, whatever the label says.

Then the back pockets. Through the 1940s and into the 1960s, rivets at the back pocket corners were placed under the fabric rather than on top, to stop them destroying saddles and chair seats. From the outside you see only a faint circular impression. It is an inconvenient detail to fake and a reliable one to trust.

Fades are the last thing to read, and the hardest to forge convincingly. Genuine wear concentrates where a body creases the cloth: honeycombs behind the knee, whiskers at the hip, a wallet outline that no laundry has removed. The pattern is asymmetric, because people are. Uniform, symmetrical fading is a factory pretending to be a decade.

Care · 5 min · January

The case against dry cleaning almost everything

Dry cleaning is a solvent bath followed by heat and mechanical agitation. It is very good at removing oil-based stains and quite good at removing the natural oils that keep wool fibres flexible. Do it four times a year for a decade and you will have a jacket that looks thin at the elbows and feels like paper, which is exactly what has happened to most of the mid-century tailoring we are shown.

For wool, the answer is almost always air and a brush. Hang the garment somewhere with moving air for a day after wearing it; most odour is volatile and simply leaves. Brush with a natural-bristle clothes brush, always downward, to lift the dust that abrades fibres from within. Steam — a kettle, a bathroom, a hand steamer held at a distance — releases creases without pressing shine into the cloth.

Spot-clean rather than committing the whole garment. A damp cloth, cold water and patience will take out more than people expect, and a specialist can treat a single mark for a fraction of what a full clean costs the fabric.

Send a piece for cleaning when it is genuinely soiled, at the end of a season before storage, or when a stain has set. That is perhaps once every two or three years. Between times, the best thing you can do for an old coat is hang it on a broad wooden hanger and leave it alone.
